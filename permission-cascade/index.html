Chapter 06 · Restrict · 12 min

The Permission Cascade

Interrupt relapse earlier by identifying the sequence of permissions before the final action.

The decisive moment often occurs before the substance appears.

The problem

Relapse is often described as one dramatic decision. In practice, the decisive moment may happen earlier: the first private exception, the first hidden message, the first romanticized memory, the first rule quietly downgraded from requirement to suggestion.

By the time the substance appears, several permissions may already be stacked behind it.

Why it happens

Permission works by sounding reasonable in small pieces. “I am doing better” may be true. “The old rules may no longer apply” may feel adult. “One exception would prove I am in control” may sound like confidence. “I do not need to tell anyone” may look like privacy. Each step weakens the next safeguard.

The cascade is dangerous because no single thought has to sound outrageous. The system fails through a sequence of plausible moves.

Operating principle

Why early permission matters

The first permission is usually more valuable than the final excuse because it still has room around it. Early in the cascade, you may not have purchased anything, messaged anyone, driven anywhere, hidden information, or crossed a line. The system can still be restored with less damage.

That is why the first permission should not be dismissed as “just a thought.” Thoughts do not need to be punished, but some thoughts should activate safeguards. “I am doing better, so maybe the old rules are excessive” is not a crime. It is a maintenance signal. “I could test control” is not proof that relapse is inevitable. It is a sign to stop testing and restore friction.

The cascade also shows why secrecy deserves special attention. Privacy can be healthy. Secrecy that protects a high-risk sequence is different. If the plan depends on nobody knowing, the plan may already be using isolation as infrastructure.

Where to interrupt the sequence

Each step has a different interruption. “I am doing better” can be answered with a maintenance review, not an exception. “The old rules may no longer apply” can be answered with evidence and a reversible test, not private removal. “One exception would prove control” can be answered by refusing tests that require exposure to the exact risk being tested.

“I do not need to tell anyone” can be interrupted by sending a small message before the story becomes complete. “I can stop immediately afterward” can be interrupted by remembering that the claim cannot be verified safely from inside the sequence. “The rest of the day no longer matters” can be interrupted by the lapse protocol. “I will rebuild tomorrow” can be interrupted by repairing one safeguard now.

The cascade is not meant to make you suspicious of every thought. It is meant to make the sequence visible early enough that you can act while the cost of repair is still low.

Interrupt the cascade

  1. Find the earliest permission, not only the final action.
  2. Name the safeguard that permission weakened.
  3. Restore visibility: tell someone before secrecy becomes part of the plan.
  4. Reverse one access step immediately.
  5. Clarify the rule in writing so the same loophole is less available next time.

Failure modes

Failure Modes

  • Only reviewing the final action and missing the first permission.
  • Treating overconfidence as evidence that safeguards are obsolete.
  • Using privacy to protect a plan that needs interruption.
  • Testing control in order to feel normal.
  • Letting one lapse become permission for the rest of the day.

Build your version

Build your version

  • What was the first permission?
  • What made it sound reasonable?
  • Which safeguard had already weakened?
  • Who or what could have interrupted it?
  • What rule now needs clarification?

Translate this into your Operating Plan

Add your personal cascade to the Operating Plan. Do not write only the final danger. Write the early steps that usually look reasonable. Your cascade might begin with boredom, resentment, celebration, payday, a private search, an old song, a route change, or the thought that the system is embarrassing now.

For each step, write an interruption. If the step is romanticizing, the interruption may be reading a black-box review. If the step is secrecy, the interruption may be sending a prepared message. If the step is testing control, the interruption may be a rule that says tests involving access are not valid tests. If the step is “tomorrow,” the interruption may be repairing one safeguard immediately.

This turns the cascade from a hidden slope into a checklist of signals. The goal is not to eliminate every permission thought. The goal is to prevent permission from quietly becoming logistics.

The first permission is actionable

You do not have to wait for a dramatic final moment. A small permission can be enough reason to restore the system.

Quick test

Ask: would I be comfortable telling a safe person about this step before taking it? If the answer is no, the step may already be part of the cascade. The point is not that every private thought is dangerous. The point is that a plan requiring secrecy deserves immediate inspection.
